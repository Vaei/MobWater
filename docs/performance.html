Cost

Measured by Water → Report Cost. Editor estimates, worst case, with all eight wave slots unrolled.

These are the editor's own estimates on whatever preview platform is active, and they were taken under SM5. The editor over-reports and it is not the number the platform's compiler produces. Treat them as a baseline to hold steady, and measure your own under the ES3.1 preview before quoting them anywhere.

The real cost is fill

None of the numbers below is the real cost. Water is a translucent plane and the bill is fill: the pixels it covers. A pond across the courtyard is nearly free; an ocean filling the screen is a full screen pass of a thousand-instruction shader. Size bodies to what they actually are, and give a decorative one a cull distance.

There is no depth prepass on this renderer, which is why fill is the whole model. Two bodies overlapping pay twice for the pixels they share.

Six samplers for six texture reads' worth of work, because every tap is Shared:Wrap. The ripple materials are drawn twice per step over a 256² target between them, which is 65k pixels of an 83-instruction shader - a rounding error beside one body of water on screen. The step reads nine neighbours rather than five because a five point Laplacian carries a wave faster along the grid axes than across its diagonals, and a ring that travels far enough turns into a square.

The vertex count is high because the wave sum happens there: eight Gerstner waves, unrolled, plus the shore attenuation. It is paid per vertex of the surface mesh, so a lake's Grid Resolution and a river's Segments Along are vertex-shader settings as much as they are silhouette ones.

Five axes is thirty two combinations, but foam texture without foam is unbuildable, so twenty four are real - plus a debug instance, which is twenty five a shape. The ocean drops the foam texture as well, its coordinates being measured from a shoreline it does not have, so it is sixteen and a debug.

The gradient is the one axis that costs almost nothing to be on: two instructions and no extra sampler, because the ramp tap replaces the absorption's exponential and reads through the same shared sampler as every other tap. It is still a permutation rather than a blend, because a body grading by absorption should not carry a tap for a palette it never reads.

What the baked sea costs

Measured against the same variant on the Disc set, which is the same shader without it:

VertexPixelSamplers
MI_MobWater_Disc_Ripples_Foam_Refraction92812297
MI_MobWater_Ocean_Ripples_Foam_Refraction99512847

Sixty seven vertex instructions, fifty five pixel, and no extra sampler. Four texture reads for two of them - two frames of displacement in the vertex shader and two of normal in the pixel one - and every read is Shared:Wrap, so none of them spends a sampler slot. That is what it costs to replace eight sines with a few hundred components.

It is a shape rather than a sixth variant axis for the reason the others are axes: a spectrum tap on every pond in the level, to serve the one body that has a spectrum, is exactly the cost this plugin is arranged around. Seventeen extra instances is the cheaper end of that trade.

Every combination a level places is a distinct shader and a distinct PSO. That is the price of not paying for features a body does not use, and it is a real price on a platform that compiles at load.

1.2 MB for a project of lakes and ponds, nearly all of it the two render targets - and the field is a fixed cost whatever a level does, because its price is its resolution.

7.5 MB once an ocean loads a sea state. Two uncompressed atlases and the table the query reads, and only a body whose Spectrum is set brings them. Uncompressed is not a choice: every block format quantises three channels together, so a compressed displacement map moves a vertex to wherever the block's average said, and what that produces is a straight seam across the whole ocean. Resolution, frame count and tile size are all bake parameters at the top of mob_water_spectrum.py; halving the resolution is a quarter of the memory.

Ripple Extent trades reach against fineness at no cost either way. Two thousand across 256 is about eight centimetres a texel; doubling the reach halves the detail and changes nothing about the bill.

The CPU side

A query is eight waves unrolled plus four walk-back iterations, per point. A pontoon array of eight points is eight of those. GetWaterInfoAtLocations saves the body lookup, not the maths.

Cpu Queries off removes it entirely for a body, which is what a decorative ocean wants.

Where the savings are

  1. Screen coverage. Everything else is a rounding error beside it.
  2. Cull distance on every body that is scenery.
  3. Ripples off on any body nothing can reach. It is five samples a pixel.
  4. Refraction off, which removes the scene colour read entirely and is the right look for deep or stylized water anyway.
  5. Foam off on bodies with no visible edge - an ocean seen only at distance.
  6. Fewer combinations, so a level compiles fewer shaders.
  7. Cpu Queries off on anything nothing floats on.
