Ships in it

The CPU query, what it answers, and what it deliberately does not know.

The API

FMobWaterInfo Info;
if (UMobWaterStatics::GetWaterInfoAtLocation(this, Location, Info))
{
    // Info.SurfaceZ, Info.Normal, Info.ImmersionDepth, Info.Depth, Info.FlowVelocity, Info.Fold,
    // Info.Exclusion

Function
GetWaterInfoAtLocationthe whole answer at one point
GetWaterSurfaceZthe surface height and nothing else. The cheap case, and the common one
GetWaterInfoAtLocationsmany points at once. A pontoon array asks about four to eight points almost always in the same body, so this looks the body up once instead of once per point
GetWaterTimethe instant the waves are being evaluated at, folded into the loop period
EvaluateWavePreseta named preset at a named instant, with no world state in the way. This is what the parity test drives
EvaluateSpectruma named baked sea state at a named instant, read out of its table rather than its texture. What the spectrum's parity test drives

What comes back

Field
bValidfalse when the point is outside every body, in which case nothing else is meaningful
SurfaceZworld Z of the surface directly above the point
Normalthe surface normal there. Straight up on a still body
ImmersionDepthhow far the point is below the surface. Negative above it
Depthhow deep the water is here - surface to bed, not surface to the point. This is the body's Depth property, answered rather than measured
FlowVelocityhow fast the surface is moving across the ground
Foldhow hard the surface is folding, 0 flat and 1 about to break. The same number crest foam is drawn from, so a splash spawned where this is high lands on a crest that actually looks like it is breaking
Exclusionhow much water an exclusion volume keeps out here, 0 none and 1 all of it. Already applied to ImmersionDepth, so nothing has to multiply by it - a point that is fully excluded answers bValid false instead

Who is asking

The first argument is the querier, and it is explicit rather than a world context that happened to be convenient.

When a ship and a client disagree about where the water is, the first thing worth knowing is almost never that the water disagreed. It is that the two machines asked about different places, because the ship itself was somewhere different. Separating those needs the query attributed to something, and attributing it to whichever object supplied the world would be right by luck and wrong from a Blueprint library.

The compounding case is the one that needs it. A character standing on a ship derives its query point from the ship's transform, so a small error in the hull becomes a large error at the feet, and presents as the water being wrong in one spot. It is not a water bug and no amount of looking at the waves finds it.

Why it can be trusted

The surface is a pure function of position and time. Two machines given the same instant compute the same answer with the same arithmetic - the CPU path and the shader are one algorithm written twice, and Verify Contract asserts they still agree. The last run: 384 points across 3 instants and 2 body scales, worst disagreement 0.00099 cm.

Exclusion is checked the same way and separately, because it is not one algorithm written twice - it is a publish path with a query on the other side of it. See the two answers checked against each other.

So buoyancy on the client and buoyancy on the server are the same calculation, and the only thing that can part them is the clock. Bind a synchronised one.

That is asserted rather than argued. A dedicated server and two clients each record the surface at eight fixed points every frame and the files are compared row by row - three machines, asserted.

What it does not know

Ripples, wakes and stamped foam are not in the answer, and cannot be. The field they live in is a render target, and a dedicated server has no GPU to hold one. Waves are shared; ripples are decoration.

Two more, each worth knowing before something is built on it:

Depth is authoredthe bed is only knowable per pixel, from the depth buffer, and the CPU cannot read it. Depth is the body's own property, so a pool sitting over a floor it does not match will report water that is not there
Exclusion is answered from every volumenot only the four the shader can see, so a query and a pixel can disagree about a distant hull - the query is right and the pixel is out of slots

Cost, and turning it off

Evaluation is per point per call: eight waves unrolled, plus four iterations walking back to the column asked about. A pontoon array of eight points is eight of those, and GetWaterInfoAtLocations saves the body lookup, not the maths.

Cpu Queries off makes a body scenery. It draws, and nothing can ask where its surface is. A decorative ocean on the horizon has no business paying for a wave evaluation nobody reads, and that is the "full GPU, or output what a ship needs" split.

Buoyancy

Add a Mob Water Buoyancy component to anything whose root simulates physics and it floats. It is the one thing GetWaterInfoAtLocations was shaped for: a handful of points asked about in one call, on a surface both machines can compute.

Pontoons

A pontoon is a sphere with an offset and a radius, and what buoyancy wants from geometry is only how much of it is under - which one height difference answers. Left empty, four are built from the body's own bounds when play begins, so a component dropped on a crate floats the crate.

Offsetwhere it sits, in the component's space
Radiushow far above and below its centre it reaches. This is the whole of the smoothing: a small radius on a choppy sea is a body either held up completely or not at all, and it hammers
Keep the pontoons level with the centre of mass or above it. Lift applied below the centre of mass has an arm that tips the body further the moment it tilts, and on anything as tall as it is wide that term beats the righting couple the pontoons make between them. A hull that rolls over on calm water is this and nothing else.

Buoyancy Coefficient

How hard the water pushes back, as a multiple of the body's own weight - not a density and a displaced volume. A sphere's volume is not the volume of the hull it stands for, so a physical model would have to be retuned every time a pontoon moved, and it answers a question nobody asks.

This one answers the question everybody asks, because the equilibrium falls straight out of it: at rest the pontoons settle at 1/Coefficient submerged. 2 puts their centres exactly on the waterline, 4 leaves them a quarter under, and below 1 the body sinks however deep the water is.

That is asserted rather than claimed. A 1 m cube on a flat body, dropped from 2 m, settles at Z 5000.0000 with submersion 0.50000 over a surface at 5000 - the predicted equilibrium to four decimals.

The rest

Vertical Dampinga rate, per second: at full submersion this is how many times a second the vertical speed is taken away. A body clear of the water is not damped at all, which is why a crate dropped from a height bounces for longer than the number suggests
Lateral Dampingthe same, sideways. What stops a raft sliding forever, and what carries it on a current
Angular Dampingthe same, turning
Flow Strengthhow much of the water's flow the body ends up travelling at. 0 is moored
Align To Surfacetips the body by the surface normal as well as by its pontoon heights. What makes a small boat lie along a swell rather than straddle it, and wrong on anything far larger than the waves

No damping force is ever larger than the one that stops its share of the body this frame. Past that it reverses the velocity into a larger one and reverses that next frame, and a boat that was floating quietly leaves the level on the first hitch - buoyancy is a stiff spring, and a stiff spring integrated forwards is only stable while the frame is short.

mob.Water.Buoyancy 1 draws every pontoon and the surface height it found. 2 also logs.

BP_MobWaterCrate in MobWater/Demo is a floating crate with nothing authored on it: drag it in over water and press play. It is what the component does with no settings touched.

This makes a claim about the water and not about the physics. Two machines given the same water and the same forces still diverge, because a solver is not deterministic across builds or frame rates. What this removes is water as a cause of it.

Debugging a disagreement

mob.Water.Debug dumps the clock raw and folded, whether a time source is bound or the engine fallback is being used, which parameter collection is in play, the ripple field's origin and stamp count, and the whole wave set wave by wave.

Run it on both machines and diff. A clock divergence shows in one line, and so does a wave set that only reached one of them. If both match, the water is not what disagreed - compare the query points next.
