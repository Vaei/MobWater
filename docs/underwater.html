Underwater

A plane in front of the camera that absorbs by distance, with the waterline drawn across it and the light coming down through it.

What it is

Attach a Mob Water Underwater component to a camera. It is a plane held just in front of the near clip, drawn only while the camera is under water, that reads the depth buffer and absorbs by how far the light travelled to reach the eye.

Attaching it is normally done for you. With Auto Underwater on, the plane rides a small actor of its own that follows whichever camera is drawing the picture: attached to the local player's camera manager while one is driving it, to the debug camera the moment it takes over, and placed at an editor viewport when nothing is playing at all.

Its own actor, and not a component on the camera manager, for a reason worth knowing if you attach one by hand: a play world will not draw anything a hidden actor owns, and APlayerCameraManager is hidden. A plane parented straight to one is submerged, fires its events and is never drawn.

A plane rather than a post-process pass, for the same reason the lights in MobLights are meshes: this renderer has no spare full-screen pass, and a material on a quad is one the renderer already knows how to draw. No scene view extension, no render graph pass, no global shader.

Absorbing by distance is the whole point. A flat overlay tints everything the same amount, so a hand in front of your face is as green as the far wall and nothing reads as being underwater - it reads as a green filter. Reading depth costs one tap and gets the whole effect.

The waterline is a line

The plane is a real quad in the world, so where the water's surface crosses it is a real line - and the surface normal the query already returned tilts that line as a swell passes. That is the whole difference between a camera at the waterline and a screen going green.

Above the line the plane is transparent and you see the world; below it, the absorption. On the line itself sits the meniscus: a bead a couple of centimetres tall, denser and brighter than the water behind it, because water clinging to a lens is the one part of the view that is genuinely thick.

Fading the whole screen in over a few centimetres instead is the same information thrown away. The camera is never wholly in or wholly out at the moment it matters, and the one thing everybody recognises about that moment is where the line was.

The surface is taken as a plane through the eye's own column, tilted by the normal there. Exact to first order, which across a quad held at arm's length is exact - and it costs a dot product where evaluating the wave set a second time in the pixel shader would cost the whole set.

Light coming down

Turn Caustics on and the same two layers the surface throws onto its bed are read where the view ray ends, and added to the water's own colour.

Added to the water rather than to what is behind it, which is the only place a translucent pass can put it - and it is also the honest one: what is being seen from down here is the web scattering in the column, not a pattern painted on a wall.

It is faded twice. By how deep the eye is, because caustics are focused light and focus is the first thing a column takes; and by how far off the thing being looked at is, because at the far end of the clarity the water has already absorbed everything.

A compiled permutation, not a strength of zero. It is two texture reads on a quad that covers the screen, which is the most expensive place in this renderer to carry something nobody asked for. Off, the taps and their coordinates are not in the shader at all - the plane runs the plain M_MobWaterUnderwater rather than MI_MobWaterUnderwater_Caustics.

Snell's window

Water bends light, so everything above the surface reaches a submerged eye inside a disc of about forty-nine degrees around the up direction. The whole hemisphere is compressed into that circle, and beyond it the surface is a mirror. It is the one cue that says the eye is under water rather than behind a coloured pane, because it is a change of geometry - nothing done to the colour stands in for it.

Turn Snell Window on and the plane draws it. The disc is not authored: its edge is the point where the surface stops transmitting, which falls out of the refractive index and nothing else. At 1.333 that is 48.6 degrees, and Verify Contract asserts it there.

Where the world above comes from

Sky refracts the same long-latitude sky the surface reflects, through the same rotation, so the window and the reflection cannot disagree about which way the level's sun is. One texture read. It is correct for the case the window is famous for - open water under an open sky, where nearly everything in the disc is sky - and what it cannot show is anything standing at the water's edge, because a sky texture has never heard of the level.

Scene Capture is the world itself: a capture looking straight up from the waterline over the eye, written into RT_MobWaterSnell and read back through the refracted ray. It is a second view of the scene and costs what a second view costs. It exists only while the eye is under the surface and is torn down on the frame it comes up.

The capture is locked to world up with no yaw, and that is load-bearing rather than a simplification. A capture that turned with the eye would have to hand its view matrix to the shader, and there are sixteen floats in one against thirty six in the whole per-instance contract. Fixed, a direction becomes a coordinate in two multiplies.

It sits at the surface rather than at the eye, because light bends where it leaves the water and a capture held down at the eye would be looking up through the water it exists to see past. Every registered body of water is hidden from it, so a wave crest overhead is not a ceiling over the view.

One render target, so one eye at a time gets a captured window. Split screen shares it, and the second view would be reading the first view's sky. That is why Sky is the default and the capture is asked for.

The disc is physics, not a fade

Transmission through the surface uses the full Fresnel equations rather than Schlick's approximation, and that is the difference between a window and a vignette. Schlick has no critical angle: it climbs smoothly past it and would leave the rim transmitting a third of the sky where the surface has in fact stopped transmitting anything. The full form reaches zero exactly where the refracted ray goes horizontal, so the edge of the disc is the physics and not a number somebody tuned.

The sky is also absorbed on the way down. The window is not a hole in the water: the column the light came through takes it like any other, so the disc dims and goes with depth on its own. Nothing needs a depth cutoff.

Outside the disc the surface mirrors the water below, and MobWater does not render that mirror - what a second downward view would show is already behind this quad, absorbed by distance. Mirror Strength thickens the water there instead, which is what makes the disc read as a disc. Set it to 0 and the water outside the window is left exactly as it was.

Hooking the camera going under

The camera crossing the surface is its own event, separate from any character crossing it, and the two want different effects. A swimmer with their head under is in water and the splash belongs to them; in anything but a first person game the camera is metres away from that, and what belongs to it is what happens to a lens - droplets on surfacing, spatter, a wipe, a muffle on the mix.

Bind it on the subsystem:

OnViewSubmergedChanged(bool bSubmerged, float DeepestImmersion). The camera crossing the surface, either way
IsViewSubmerged()whether the camera is under
GetViewSubmersion()0 above, 1 fully under. The crossfade value
GetViewImmersionDepth()how far below the surface the camera is, and zero above it
GetViewDeepestImmersion()the deepest it reached without coming up, kept after it has
GetViewUnderwater()the plane itself, for anything the rest does not answer
DeepestImmersion is there because depth is the wrong question at the moment of surfacing - it is nothing by then. How wet a lens should be is a question about where the camera has just been, so scale the droplets by that and let them dry off on your own timer. It is cleared on going back under, not on coming up.

Bind the subsystem and not the plane's own OnSubmergedChanged wherever the plane is one the plugin attached. The plane is rebuilt every time the view changes hands - a debug camera taking over, a play starting, a viewport taking the picture back - and a binding on it goes with it. The subsystem re-broadcasts and outlives all of that, including saying false on the way out if the view is taken away while it is still under.

The component's own hooks are still there for a plane a project attached itself:

IsSubmerged()whether the camera is under
GetSubmersion()0 above, 1 fully under. The crossfade value
GetImmersionDepth()how far below the surface, and zero above it
GetDeepestImmersion()the deepest it reached without coming up
OnSubmergedChangedbind sound, audio filtering, breath, whatever a project does when the head goes under
The submersion test asks the same query everything else does, so the camera goes under at the height the waves actually put the surface, not at the body's still level. A swell passing over a swimmer's head submerges the view.
Droplets, spatter and any other lens effect are the project's own Niagara or material, broadcast the way splashes are. MobWater says when, not what - a lens is a look, and a look belongs to whoever is making the game.

What is not done

The mirror outside Snell's window is not drawn. Past the critical angle the surface reflects the water below, and that would want a second view pointing down as well as the one pointing up. What is there instead is the water itself, thickened, which is most of what the mirror shows in anything but a swimming pool.
The window is refracted through one surface normal, the one at the eye, so the disc tilts with the swell but does not ripple. A real one shimmers because the surface over it is rough at every scale, and that would want the ripple field sampled per pixel across the whole window rather than once.

The caustics the surface draws are a different thing again: those are thrown downwards onto the bed, which is the view from above. See Caustics.

Cost

Measured under Report Cost. It covers the whole screen while it is drawn, which is the only time anything in this plugin does - but it is drawn only while the camera is submerged, and while it is, most of what it is covering is water that has stopped being drawn behind it.
