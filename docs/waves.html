Waves

A sum of Gerstner waves, evaluated identically on the CPU and the GPU, from a clock you supply.

A still body is not a small ocean

Wave parameters are per body, and that is the difference between water that reads as a pond and water that reads as a bad ocean. A pond has ripples and no swell, because nothing in it has the fetch to build one. A lake has a long low chop. Only the sea gets the sea's waves.

Three presets ship, and a newly placed body takes the one for its shape:

PresetWavesLongestTallestChoppiness
WP_MobWater_Pond22.6 m1.6 cm0.25
WP_MobWater_Lake39 m7 cm0.5
WP_MobWater_Ocean540 m85 cm1.0
Getting this wrong is the single loudest way for enclosed water to read as wrong, and no amount of tuning the amplitude fixes it. A pond carrying an ocean's swell is not a calmer ocean, it is a pond with the wrong wavelengths in it.

One wave

Directionwhich way it travels, on the horizontal plane. Normalised on use
Wavelengthcrest to crest, and also what decides how fast it travels
Amplitudestill surface to crest
Steepnesshow far the crest leans forward. 0 is a plain sine, 1 is as sharp as it gets without folding
Phase Offsetwhere in its cycle it starts. Two waves sharing a direction need this to differ or they are one wave
There is no speed on a wave, deliberately. In deep water a wave's phase speed is fixed by its length, so a set with an authored speed per wave can be given values no sea produces - and it reads as wrong before anyone can say why. Speed comes out of the dispersion relation, from gravity and the wavelength.

The set

Amplitude Scalescales every wave's height
Speed Scalescales how fast they travel. 0 freezes the surface without flattening it
Choppiness Scalescales how far crests lean. 0 leaves sines, which is what a small still body wants

The three scalars exist so one preset can serve a pond and a harbour at different amplitudes, rather than two sets that drift apart the first time one is tuned. A body then scales again with its own Wave Amplitude and Wave Speed - the same authored waves at a third the height and half the speed, which is what a harbour is next to the sea it opens onto.

Those two share one slot of a body's per-instance data, which is full at the engine's thirty six floats: the amplitude is the whole part in hundredths and the speed is the fraction. It is why the amplitude is quantised to a hundredth and the speed tops out at five. The query packs and unpacks the same float the shader reads, so neither can answer a surface the other is not drawing.
Neither touches the baked sea. Its frequencies are quantised to whole turns over the loop, which is the only reason it loops at all, and a body running it faster would be running a sea that no longer meets itself. Scale a baked sea on the spectrum asset instead.
Steepness is shared out across the set rather than applied per wave, so adding a ninth wave to a calm sea does not fold it. Eight waves is the ceiling: the shader unrolls to that count, so raising it is a shader change and not only a data one.

Waves lie down at the bank

Amplitude is weighted by how far a point is from the body's own edge, smoothly, over Shore Fade Distance. Without it a lake's waves keep their full height as the bank comes up and stand above dry ground at the rim. It is one multiply, and it is the difference between an edge that reads as a shoreline and one that reads as a plane intersecting terrain.

Distance to the edge, and not the depth of the water, however much the second sounds like the better measure. The displacement happens in the vertex shader, and a vertex shader cannot read the depth buffer - the bed is only recoverable per pixel, by which point the vertex has already moved. The edge of a box, a disc or a spline is arithmetic, so the vertex shader and the CPU query can both have it. Scene depth still does everything it can do: colour, clarity and shoreline foam are all per pixel.

The baked sea

A Gerstner set is a handful of sines, so it can only ever look like a handful of sines. Real open water is a continuum, and the crossing patterns that make it read as an ocean rather than as a corrugated sheet come from hundreds of components at once. Those cannot be summed per vertex on this renderer, so they are summed once at author time and sampled instead.

Set a body's Spectrum - Ocean only - and it is added to whatever the wave preset already carries. An ocean whose spectrum has not been baked still has waves rather than turning to glass, and a level can put a long authored swell under a baked chop.

SP_MobWater_Ocean
Grid64² texels over a 61 m tile, so a texel is 96 cm
Loop128 frames over 16 seconds
Sea statePhillips at 6 m/s, normalised to 35 cm rms - about 1.4 m significant wave height
Weighttwo 1056×528 atlases at 2.13 MB each, and 2.00 MB of query table

It loops exactly

Every component's angular frequency is rounded down to a whole number of turns over the loop period before the transform runs. That one line is what makes the last frame the frame before the first: there is no crossfade anywhere, and no seam to find. A seam in time on an ocean is more obvious than a seam in space, because the eye is looking for a rhythm rather than for a line.

It also makes the bake fast. Every phase factor is then an exact root of unity, so the inner loop is a table read and there is no transcendental in it - the whole solve takes about six seconds in the editor's own Python, with no numpy anywhere.

The price is that the longest components travel a few per cent slow, because their speed has been rounded to the nearest available harmonic. A longer Loop Period has more harmonics to round onto and costs proportionally more frames.

Texels against frames

Resolution and frame count are not independent. A wave's speed comes from its length, so the shortest wave the grid can hold is also the fastest, and a bake with more texels than frames strobes - the small chop teleports between frames instead of travelling. 64 texels across 61 m is a wave every 1.9 m at 1.1 s a cycle, and 128 frames over the 16 s loop samples that eight times a cycle. Doubling the resolution without doubling the frames would halve that and the surface would crawl.

Anything finer than the grid belongs in the scrolling detail normals, which cost no memory and do not have to loop. The spectrum is cut off just above two and a half texels for exactly that reason: energy the grid cannot carry does not disappear, it bakes as noise and then strobes.

The query reads the same bytes

Not the texture. A dedicated server's texture is a GPU upload that may never have been made, so the asset carries the displacement a second time as a plain byte table, and that is what GetWaterInfoAtLocation reads. Two implementations of a Gerstner sum can be asserted equal; two solves of a Fourier transform in two languages cannot, and the networking story needs the same answer rather than nearly the same one.

Verify Contract renders the atlas through a probe and compares it against the table. The last run: 192 points across 3 instants, worst disagreement 0.05458 cm. That is a filter weight and not a difference of maths - both sides read the same bytes, and what is left is that a GPU's bilinear weights are fixed point.

It found a real fault at ten times that figure, and the fault was the sampler rather than the maths. The frames sit side by side in one atlas, and the shared wrap sampler is anisotropic - so a surface seen at a grazing angle was averaging in the neighbouring frame, which on a travelling wave comes to nothing at all and read as the sea flattening towards the horizon. The taps name their mip level now, which is what makes a widening filter impossible.

The clock

Water time is an input. MobWater ships no clock of its own, because a clock that two machines agree on is a project's problem and every project has already solved it differently.

UMobWaterSubsystem::Get(World)->SetTimeSource(
    FMobWaterTimeSource::CreateUObject(this, &UMyClock::GetSyncedTime));

Unbound, it falls back to AGameStateBase::GetServerWorldTimeSeconds, which is enough to see water move and not enough to keep two machines in phase - it is corrected in steps, and a step in the clock is a step in every wave at once. Outside a game world it accumulates locally, which is what makes the viewport move without playing.

What to bind is a clock corrected by rate rather than by snapping. Vorixo's non-destructive synced net clock is the reference implementation: it never moves time backwards and never jumps it forwards, it dilates until it has caught up, so a correction arriving mid-swell is invisible on the surface instead of being a frame where every wave moved at once.

Folding

Whatever the source says is folded into Time Loop Period - five minutes by default - before anything reads it, and the same folded value reaches the material and every CPU query. Two reasons, and both are load-bearing:

  • A session long enough leaves a float no fraction to work with, and the waves quantise into visible steps while nothing about the clock looks wrong.
  • If the pixels and the physics read different clocks, buoyancy sits slightly off the surface it is drawn on, and no amount of tuning the waves closes it.

GetWaterTime is the folded value. Anything reproducing a surface later - a replay, a server checking a client's claim - wants that rather than the world's own time.

Three machines, asserted

Everything else here is asserted on one machine, and one machine cannot fail the claim the plugin rests on: that a server and a client handed the same instant compute exactly the same surface. That claim is only about two.

python Python/mob_water_determinism.py --engine <engine> --project <project.uproject>

It launches a real dedicated server and two real clients that connect to it, has each spawn its own body of water and record the surface at eight fixed points every frame, and compares the files. Three runs, and the second is the one that matters:

The same clockall three handed the same instant. Nothing but an exact match is accepted, because there is nowhere for a difference to come from
The clock steppedthe three start at 0, at one loop period, and at a thousand of them - a raw clock of eighty hours. Folded, all three are the same instant, so the surfaces have to be identical again. That is what proves the fold: a wrap out by anything at all leaves a machine an hour into a session drawing a different sea from one that just started, which is not a bug anybody finds by playing
The controltwo machines a third of a loop apart, which must disagree. Without it the harness passes just as happily on a recorder that writes the same row every frame

Last run: 400 frames on each of three machines, every row identical to the last digit of a double, and the control differed.

The synthetic clock is the point rather than a shortcut. A real clock differs between machines by design, so a comparison against one would be measuring the clock instead of the water and would fail for a reason nobody could fix. Handed the same instant, the two sides have nothing left to disagree about but the arithmetic.
Its step is a sixty fourth of a second, not a sixtieth, because a sixty fourth is exactly representable in binary. That is not tidiness: a machine eighty hours into a session rounds a clock of 288000.0166 differently from one that rounds 0.0166, and the comparison would then fail on the precision of the number it was handed rather than on the fold. That failure is real - the fold cannot recover precision the raw time has already lost - and it is a reason to hand in a clock that is not enormous, not a reason to distrust the wrap.

The same maths twice

MobWaterWaves.h and Shaders/Public/MobWaterWaves.ush are one algorithm written twice, because a vertex shader cannot call C++ and a dedicated server has no GPU. Nothing but a test keeps them equal.

Water → Verify Contract evaluates both at sampled points and instants and reports the worst disagreement. The last run: 384 points across 3 instants and 2 body scales, worst disagreement 0.00099 cm. The baked tier is checked the same way and agrees to 0.05458 cm, which is looser for a reason the section above gives.

Phase is folded on both terms before they meet, and that is not an optimisation. A world position a kilometre out times a wave number is a phase in the thousands of radians; a float carries about seven digits, so the sine of it quantises and the surface reads as faceted far from the origin and smooth near it. Folding the distance by the wavelength and the time term by a full turn is exact, because both are periods of the wave itself.

Where the surface is

A Gerstner wave moves a point sideways as well as up, so the height above a world XY is not the height of the vertex that started there. The query walks back towards the column it was asked about four times - a fixed count rather than a tolerance, so two machines take the same path to the same answer.
